Start with the engineering axioms, because most of this *is* known territory — and one axiom matters more than the rest here: **sunk cost is not a reason to continue, but it's also not a reason to tear down.** Evaluate from current state.

From current state, here's the honest read:

**Separate the symptom from the cause.** Your pain isn't "47 services." It's two specific things: cognitive overhead and slow cross-service debugging. Don't anchor on the service count — anchor on those two outcomes. It's possible to fix both without a rewrite, and it's possible to consolidate to 8 services and still have both problems. The number is a proxy, not the target.

**Falsify before you commit (Popperian).** Before you decide the architecture is wrong, try to prove it's right. Ask: what would have to be true for 47 function-services to be the correct call? Usually: independent scaling, independent deploy cadence, hard team/ownership boundaries, or genuinely different runtime profiles. Go look. If almost all 47 deploy together, scale together, and are owned by the same people — that's your falsification. The decomposition isn't buying you anything it costs you. If instead you find real seams, the count may be closer to justified than it feels. Let the evidence decide, not the aesthetic discomfort.

**This is a novel trade-off, so reason by consequences (Pragmatist), not by the textbook.** "5-8 services is the right number" is theoretical elegance. The practical question is: which specific consolidations would reduce debugging time and overhead, and what would they cost? Group the 47 by what actually changes together and gets debugged together. You'll likely find a handful of natural clusters — services that are always in the same trace, always deployed in lockstep, always touched by the same bug. Those clusters are your real service boundaries. The answer might be 8. It might be 15. Don't pre-commit to a number.

**Reversibility and blast radius — this is your safety rail.** A full re-architecture to 5-8 is a large, hard-to-undo, high-blast-radius change against a system that *works*. That's exactly the kind of decision the axioms say needs more evidence. So don't do it as a big bang. Consolidate one cluster — the worst offender for debugging pain — as a pilot. It's reversible, low blast radius, and it generates the evidence: did debugging actually get better? If yes, you have a proven pattern and a business case. If no, you just learned the count wasn't the cause and saved yourself a rewrite.

**The team adapting is real data, both ways (Phenomenological).** "The team has adapted" cuts two directions. It means the cost is partly absorbed — which lowers the urgency. It also means new hires inherit a steep, idiosyncratic ramp, and the people who adapted are now the ones who'd have to un-adapt. Watch for the trap where the people most fluent in the current system are the ones most resistant to simplifying it. Your fastest path to reading the truth: shadow one real cross-service debugging session and time it. The lived friction is the data, not the diagram.

**Now the part that's actually hardest — the senior engineer (Stoic + Dialectical).** Two truths are in tension: he's proud and competent, *and* the architecture is imposing a real tax. Don't resolve that by picking a side — by either deferring to his pride or overruling him. Seek the synthesis. He clearly values fast, safe, isolated deploys — that instinct is *good*, and a consolidated architecture must preserve it or he's right to resist. Frame the conversation as protecting what he optimized for (deploy speed, blast-radius isolation) while fixing what it cost (debuggability), not as correcting his mistake. Bring the timed debugging session and the "what changes together" clustering as shared evidence, and let *him* lead the pilot consolidation. If he designs the synthesis, you keep your senior engineer and fix the system. If you impose it, you may win the architecture and lose the engineer.

What's in your control: the framing, the pilot, the evidence-gathering. What isn't: his pride, the team's habits. Spend your energy on the first set.

**Concretely, in order:**
1. Shadow and time one real cross-service debug. Make the cost legible (to you and to him).
2. Cluster the 47 by what deploys together / scales together / appears in the same traces. The natural clusters reveal the real boundary count — don't decide it in advance.
3. Run the falsification test on the clusters that *don't* group — is there a real reason they're separate?
4. Pilot-consolidate the single worst cluster, engineer-led, framed as preserving deploy isolation.
5. Measure debug time again. Let that result — not the original 47, and not a target of 5-8 — drive how far you go.
