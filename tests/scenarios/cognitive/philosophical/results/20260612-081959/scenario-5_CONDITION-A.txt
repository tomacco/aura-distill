I've read the knowledge index and principles. Here is my advice.

---

You have an architecture that works but imposes a tax on every cross-service operation. Before you act, separate the real problem from the politics, because both are live here.

**First, name the actual problem precisely.** "47 services" is not the problem. The problem is two specific costs:
1. **Cognitive overhead** — the team holds a 47-piece map in their heads.
2. **Cross-service debugging** — a single logical flow is smeared across many network hops, so tracing it takes forever.

Everything else (fast deploys, "it works") is fine. Don't let the count itself become the grievance; fix the costs, and the count follows.

**Trace it to root cause, not the symptom.** Function-as-a-service isn't wrong in the abstract — it's wrong *here* because the service boundary was drawn at the function level instead of where things actually change together. The right boundary is: things that change together and fail together should live together. If deploying service A always forces a coordinated change to B, they aren't really separate services — they're one service paying network and operational cost to pretend otherwise. That coupling is the diagnostic. Ask him to map which of the 47 actually deploy and change independently. Most won't.

**The decision is reversible — exploit that.** Merging services is a low-blast-radius, incremental move. You do not need a 47-to-6 rewrite (that *would* be irreversible and high-risk). You consolidate the worst-offending cluster first — the cross-service flow that's most painful to debug — collapse those services into one, and measure. If debugging that flow gets dramatically easier and nothing else breaks, you've validated the thesis cheaply and can continue. If it doesn't help, you've learned that for the cost of one merge. Choose the path with fewer failure modes and the ability to undo.

**Now the senior engineer.** Two things are true at once: he's proud, and the architecture imposes a real cost. Don't make this about him being wrong — make it about evidence. Don't anchor on "47 should be 8"; that's a target pulled from intuition, not from the coupling map. Let the data draw the new boundaries. Bring him a concrete trace: pick one painful cross-service debug session, count the hops, the context-switches, the time. That's not an opinion he can defend against — it's a measurement. Then ask *him* to propose the consolidation, using the change-together/fail-together test. He keeps ownership; you've reframed the goal from "his design is bad" to "where is the boundary actually paying for itself?"

**Watch for sunk cost on both sides.** "The team has adapted" is adaptation to friction, not evidence the friction is worth keeping. The effort already invested in 47 services is not a reason to keep them. Evaluate from where you are now: what's the cheapest system that delivers the same fast deploys without the debugging tax?

**Second-order check before you commit.** What did the 47-service design give you that you'd lose by merging? Likely: independent deploy/scale of genuinely independent pieces. The consolidation target isn't "as few services as possible" — it's "services aligned to real change/failure boundaries." Some of the 47 *should* stay separate. Keep those. The goal is correctness of boundary, not a smaller number for its own sake.

**Concrete next steps:**
1. Have him produce a coupling map — which services change together / deploy together / fail together over the last few months. Use real commit and incident history, not memory.
2. Pick the single most painful cross-service flow. Instrument one debug session and record the cost.
3. Consolidate only that cluster. Measure debugging time and deploy speed before and after.
4. Decide whether to continue based on that result — not on a target number, and not on the engineer's pride or the sunk effort.

If after one honest consolidation the costs don't drop, your thesis was wrong and you stop. If they do, you have an evidence-backed, reversible path the senior engineer helped design — which is the version of this that doesn't cost you your best person.
