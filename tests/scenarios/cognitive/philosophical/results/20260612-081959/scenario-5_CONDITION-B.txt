## What you do

Don't reorganize yet. You don't have the data to justify a 47→7 migration, and "I find it confusing" is not yet a falsifiable case. Build that case first, then act surgically.

### 1. Separate what's actually broken from what's just unfamiliar

Two of your three complaints are real costs; one might not be.

- **"Deploys are fast"** — that's a genuine win of the architecture. Keep it.
- **"Cognitive overhead is massive"** — real, but partly a function of *unfamiliarity and lack of tooling*, not the granularity itself. Worth isolating.
- **"Debugging cross-service flows takes forever"** — this is your hard cost, and it's the one that compounds. This is where the money is.

The trap here is letting "I don't like how this looks" masquerade as "this is broken." Bracket your assumption that fewer services is self-evidently better, and look at what's actually failing in lived practice: people lose hours tracing a request across 12 hops. That specific pain is the data. Measure it.

### 2. Get falsifiable before you migrate

A reorg of 47 services is expensive and irreversible-ish. Don't do it on a hunch. Make the case disprovable:

- Pull the last 10–15 incidents/bugs. How many required tracing across N services? What was the mean time-to-diagnosis vs. your single-service bugs?
- Track onboarding: how long until a new hire can confidently change a cross-service flow?
- Count the deploy/ops surface: 47 services = 47 pipelines, dashboards, alert configs, dependency upgrades. That toil is quantifiable.

If the numbers show cross-service debugging and ops toil dominate, you have an evidence-backed case. If they don't, you've just saved yourself a painful migration and learned the real problem is **observability**, not boundaries. Either outcome is a win — that's the point of making it testable.

### 3. The likely synthesis: it's not 47 vs. 7

Don't frame this as "his architecture vs. my architecture." Both positions hold something true:

- **He's right** that small, independently-deployable units give fast deploys, blast-radius isolation, and clear ownership.
- **You're right** that function-per-service shreds a coherent business flow into fragments no one can hold in their head.

The synthesis is almost always **consolidate by domain boundary, not by function**. Services should be split where the *business* has a seam (orders, billing, inventory), not where a function ends. You very likely collapse the 47 into a handful of domain services *internally composed of* those same functions — you keep his modularity at the code level while restoring a debuggable unit at the deployment level. He doesn't lose his design; it moves down a layer.

That reframe also matters politically: you're not telling him he was wrong, you're telling him the right granularity for functions is the module, and the right granularity for services is the domain.

### 4. Before you touch boundaries, fix observability — and run the premortem

Two reasons to do tracing first:

1. **Distributed tracing (OpenTelemetry), correlation IDs, and a service dependency map** may kill most of the debugging pain *without any reorg*. If a trace view turns a 2-hour cross-service hunt into a 5-minute one, you may not need to consolidate much at all. Cheapest possible test of your hypothesis — do it first.
2. **Premortem the migration.** Imagine it's a year out and the consolidation failed. Why? Likely answers: you reintroduced the deploy coupling he was escaping; you merged services that *should* have stayed split and created a new mini-monolith; the team — which has *adapted* to this and is productive — lost velocity during the churn for a benefit that never materialized. Naming these now tells you exactly what to protect.

### 5. On the senior engineer

He's proud, he's competent, and the team works *because* of his system, not despite it. Treat that as a constraint to design around, not an obstacle to bulldoze.

- Bring him the incident data and ask *him* to interpret it. He may reach the domain-boundary conclusion himself — he has more context than you do, and ownership of the fix matters more than authorship of the critique.
- Frame it as evolution, not correction: the architecture got you fast deploys and clear ownership; the next problem to solve is cross-service legibility. That's a problem *worth* his skill, not an indictment of his work.
- Watch for the failure mode where his pride (or yours) drives the decision instead of the incident numbers. Whoever's ego is steering, the tracing data is the tiebreaker.

### Concrete sequence

1. Instrument: distributed tracing + correlation IDs. (Days, reversible, high-value regardless.)
2. Measure: pull incident/onboarding/ops-toil data. Define what "too granular" means in numbers.
3. Re-evaluate: if tracing fixed the pain, stop — you're done cheaply.
4. If not, map the real domain boundaries *with* the senior engineer.
5. Consolidate **one** domain as a pilot. Validate it actually reduced debug time before touching the rest. Fallibilism: don't migrate 47 services on a theory — migrate one, observe, then decide.

The honest bottom line: you may not yet know whether this architecture is wrong or just under-tooled. That's fine — the steps above are designed to tell you which, before you spend a quarter on a migration you can't easily undo.
