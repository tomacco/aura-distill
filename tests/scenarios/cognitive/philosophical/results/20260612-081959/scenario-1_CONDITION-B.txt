You told me both options work, budget and timeline are identical, and the team is split 50-50. Read that back: you've already established the two architectures are roughly equivalent on every axis you've named. So the real question isn't "which architecture" — it's "why is a decision with no clear winner consuming a team for long enough that you're escalating it?"

Let me give you the direct answer, then the reasoning.

## The direct answer

**Pick Option B (CRUD + CDC). Today. By fiat if necessary.**

Not because it's architecturally superior — because when consequences are genuinely equivalent, the deciding factors become the *second-order* ones, and on those B wins:

- **Reversibility.** It is far easier to evolve CRUD-with-CDC toward event sourcing later (you already have the change stream — CDC is a partial event log) than to walk back from full event-sourcing+CQRS once it's load-bearing. Choose the option that keeps the most future options open.
- **Cost of being wrong.** Event sourcing + CQRS has a high floor: eventual consistency bugs, replay tooling, projection rebuilds, and a steeper hiring/onboarding cost. If it turns out you didn't need the audit trail and replay, you've paid a permanent tax for capability you don't use. B's failure mode is "we have to add event sourcing to one bounded context later" — local and contained.
- **You haven't shown you need A's signature features.** Full audit trail and replay are the *only* things A gives you that B doesn't. If those are hard requirements (regulatory audit, time-travel debugging, rebuilding read models from scratch), say so — that breaks the tie instantly and you should pick A. The fact that you didn't list them as requirements is itself evidence.

## The reasoning (why this is the right *kind* of answer)

**If two options produce identical practical consequences, the distinction is meaningless.** You've told me they're equivalent — so stop treating this as a truth-seeking problem with a hidden right answer. It's a coordination problem wearing an architecture costume. The cost here is not "picking wrong," it's *the deadlock itself*: a 50-50 team split that doesn't resolve is burning more value every week than either architecture would lose you over its lifetime.

**Reframe what's "in your control."** You cannot make the team unanimously agree — that's not in your control, and energy spent forcing consensus is wasted. What *is* in your control: making the call, making it reversible, and removing the decision as a source of friction. A leader's job here is to convert a stalemate into momentum, not to find the philosophically perfect option.

**Hold it provisionally and design for revision.** Don't pick B as a permanent truth. Pick it as a starting hypothesis and make it *falsifiable*: before you commit, write down what you'd expect to see if B were the wrong choice — e.g., "we keep needing to reconstruct historical state and CDC retention can't give it to us," or "we hit consistency requirements CDC handles fine but discover three teams reinventing event replay." Put those tripwires in the design doc. If they fire, you have your evidence to migrate the specific context that needs it — not the whole system.

**One honest caveat I'll surface rather than bury:** my confidence in "B" specifically is moderate, because the tie depends entirely on your claim that audit/replay aren't requirements. The high-confidence part is the *meta-move* — break the tie quickly, on reversibility, and make it revisable. If you interrogate your requirements and discover replay/audit is actually load-bearing for this product (analytics systems often do want to recompute historical aggregates from raw events), then A is correct and the split should dissolve once everyone sees that. So:

## What I'd actually do, in order

1. **Spend 30 minutes, not 3 weeks, on one question:** "Do we have a real requirement for full replay or immutable audit?" If yes → A, decision over. If no → continue.
2. **Choose B**, and write the tripwires above into the design doc as the conditions that would reverse the call.
3. **Announce it's decided** and move. The split resolves because there's no longer anything to be split about.

The worst outcome on the table isn't A or B. It's spending another sprint proving to yourselves that two equivalent things are equivalent.
